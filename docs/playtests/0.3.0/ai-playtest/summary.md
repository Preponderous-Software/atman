### Q1. In your own words, what did you think the game was about? What did the rope and the snake, and the clay and the pots, mean?

- **s01:** An ordinary village day used to show that what we take as many separate things are names and shapes laid over one reality. The pots I made and named (Ganga, Moon, Sparrow) were, seen through the clay lens, just clay; breaking one changed nothing real, like space in a pot rejoining space. The snake on the dusk path was fear projected onto something not seen clearly; looking steadily with the lamp showed it was only ever a rope, and the fear meter dropped. Together they seemed to say the world of many things is like that snake: a mistaken appearance over the one Self, which clear seeing removes. The dream and waking, and 'Nothing more is asked of you' with V to step back and watch, felt like the waking state seen as another dream and the self as witness.
- **s02:** An ordinary afternoon in a river village used to show that the many things and selves we see are one underlying reality seen under different names and forms. The pots I made and named (Ganga, Annapurna, Chai) were all just clay under the lens: the names and shapes are real enough to use, but what they are is one thing. Breaking a pot showed the space inside it was never separate from the space outside, which the codex likens to individual selves and the one Self. The snake on the dusk path was fear coming from not seeing clearly. When I held the lamp on it, it was a rope and always had been, so the error simply stopped and nothing was destroyed.
- **s03:** A quiet day in a river village used to teach non-dual Vedanta. The pots I threw and named (a water jar, a bowl, a cup) showed up as just 'clay' through the clay lens. The names and shapes are real enough to use, but what they are made of is one thing, and breaking one let the space inside it rejoin the space outside (the codex links that to the self and Brahman). The snakes on the dusk path were fear made by poor light; with the lamp held to them and a steady look they turned out to be rope and the Fear bar dropped. The snake was never there and the rope never changed: the many-ness and the fear are an error laid over one reality, and seeing clearly removes them. The dream after sleeping, with time running backward and 'Nothing here seems strange', seemed to say the same about dreaming, but I could not tell where it was going.
- **s04:** An ordinary day in a village used to point at what does not change underneath it. The pots I named (Ganga, Moon, Sip) all read 'clay' through the clay lens, and breaking one just leaves clay: names and forms are real enough to use but the substance is one. The snake on the dusk path raised a Fear meter until I held the lamp steady on it and it was only ever a rope: the fear came from not seeing clearly, and light (knowledge) removes it without changing the rope. Sleep then walked me through waking, dream and dreamless sleep, and the closing line said I was there for all three, so the self is the witness present in every state; the codex calls it the fourth.

### Q2. Did anything feel preachy, confusing, or inaccurate (in the teaching, the verses in the codex, or how the tradition is described)? Say what and where; if nothing did, say so.

- **s01:** Nothing felt preachy: the codex only appears when you ask for it, cites sources (Chandogya Upanisad 6.1.4, Gaudapada Mandukya Karika 2.17-18 and 3.3-4) with Sanskrit, transliteration and translation, and the italic notes are short. The rope-and-snake note's line that the village, chores and people are 'that kind of error; they are real for living in' was a careful touch. I cannot judge accuracy of the Sanskrit or translations. Confusing: the dream after sleeping ('Nothing here seems strange', clock running backwards) - I could not tell what it was teaching or whether I was meant to do something; the one line of meaning I guessed (dream vs waking) was never stated. I did not open the 'Other schools' page, so cannot say how other traditions are described.
- **s02:** Nothing felt preachy. The game shows rather than lectures, and the 'Other schools' codex page fairly gives the Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja) and Dvaita (Madhva) readings of 'tat tvam asi' and says the game follows one school. Small confusions: that page mentions the snake and rope before I had met them at dusk, which is a mild spoiler. The pot-space and rope-snake pages say their translations are a 'working translation ... awaiting review', so I could not judge their accuracy. I am not qualified to judge the Sanskrit; nothing looked wrong to me.
- **s03:** Not preachy: the codex is careful, gives Sanskrit with transliteration and sources, and the 'Other schools' page fairly sets out Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita readings of 'tat tvam asi', saying plainly that the game follows one reading and that this is not 'what Hinduism says'. I do not have the expertise to judge the translations, and I saw nothing obviously inaccurate. What confused me was the dream: the prompt promised sleep until morning, but I got a backward-running clock and 'Nothing here seems strange' with no explanation in the HUD or in a codex page I found. The 'Other schools' page also mentions the snake and rope before you meet them, which is a small spoiler, and its text is very small to read.
- **s04:** Nothing felt preachy; the game mostly shows rather than tells, and the 'Other schools' codex page fairly notes that Visistadvaita and Dvaita read 'tat tvam asi' differently and that Atman teaches Advaita as one reading. The confusing part was the dream: 'Nothing here seems strange' with a clock running backward made me think I had to find something, and the black deep-sleep screen looked like a crash; only the waking caption and 'The dream' codex page made it make sense afterward. I cannot judge the Sanskrit or translations myself; they cite Hume and sources, which inspired trust.
