### Q1. In your own words, what did you think the game was about? What did the rope and the snake, and the clay and the pots, mean?

- **s01:** I took it to be a quiet daily-life game about seeing past names and forms to what things are made of. The clay and the pots were clear to me. I named three pots Ganga, Annam and Chai, and through the clay lens each one read '<name>: clay'. After I broke one, the codex said the space inside a broken pot was never separate from the space outside. So the pots stand for separate selves or things, which are really one stuff (Brahman) under different names. I never met the snake or the rope in play, because the dusk-path step never fired. From the codex line 'as the snake is put on the rope' I would guess the snake is the fear and separateness we project onto something harmless, and the rope is what is really there, but that comes from the codex, not from playing.
- **s02:** An ordinary afternoon in a river village used to show that the many things we name are one underlying reality seen under different names and forms. The pots I threw and named (Ganga, Annapurna, Diya) were, through the clay lens, just 'clay': the names and shapes are real enough to use but nothing other than the clay. Breaking a pot showed that the space inside was never separate from the space outside (the codex's 'space in the pot' page), i.e. the individual self is not separate from the Self. The rope and the snake: in the dark at dusk my fear rose because I saw a snake; looking closely with the lamp showed it was always a rope, and the fear went. It stands for mistaking the world/self for something it is not through ignorance, and knowledge (not doing anything to the rope) removing the error. The codex also made clear the village itself is not to be treated as a hallucination like the snake.
- **s03:** An ordinary village afternoon used to show how we mistake names and appearances for what is real. The pots I threw and named turned out, through the clay lens, to be just clay: the name and shape are added on, and the clay is what is really there. The snake on the dusk path was my fear projected onto a rope in poor light. When I looked at it steadily with the lamp, the snake simply wasn't there and fear fell to zero. The codex ties this to the Self: the world of names is laid over one reality, as the snake is laid over the rope. I read it as being about seeing clearly rather than escaping the world, since the codex says the village and its chores are not that kind of error.
- **s04:** A quiet village afternoon about seeing what things really are. The pots I threw and named (Ganga, Kashi, Lota) showed up as just 'clay' through the clay lens, and breaking one changed nothing about the clay: names and shapes are real enough to use, but what they are is one thing. I never reached the snake and rope scene (stuck on the dusk path), so I only know it from the codex: the fear of a snake that is really a rope, the error being put on top of what is there. I took it to be about the self: you think you are a separate thing, as the space in a pot seems separate, but it is the same as all space.

### Q2. Did anything feel preachy, confusing, or inaccurate (in the teaching, the verses in the codex, or how the tradition is described)? Say what and where; if nothing did, say so.

- **s01:** Nothing felt preachy. The teaching came through doing (naming, the lens, breaking a pot), and the codex is restrained. The 'Other schools' page fairly sets out Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita readings of tat tvam asi, and notes that Atman teaches one school's reading, which I appreciated. A few things were confusing rather than inaccurate. The 'Other schools' page spoils the snake/rope image before the dusk path. 'The space in the pot' carries a placeholder credit ('Working translation ... awaiting review'), which undercuts trust in the verse. The 'see RESEARCH.md' references mean nothing to a player. I cannot judge the accuracy of the Sanskrit or the translations.
- **s02:** Not preachy: the teaching is shown through play and the codex is measured; the 'Other schools' page fairly sets Shankara's Advaita beside Ramanuja and Madhva and says outright that the game teaches one school's reading, not 'what Hinduism says', which I appreciated. Confusing bits were presentational: the codex lines 'Working translation for Atman from the Sanskrit, awaiting review' and 'see RESEARCH.md' read like unfinished dev notes and made me unsure whether to trust those translations; the Advaita summary mentions 'the snake is put on the rope' before the player has met the rope; and the clay lens effect itself was hidden behind the HUD, so the 'it is all clay' moment came more from the MADE list text ('Ganga: clay') than from seeing it. I could not judge the accuracy of the Sanskrit or translations myself.
- **s03:** Nothing felt preachy; the teaching comes through actions, and the codex is opt-in. The 'Other schools' page was a welcome surprise. It gives Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita readings of 'tat tvam asi', notes the grammar alone does not settle it, and says the game follows one school's reading, not 'what Hinduism says'. That felt fair. What confused me was mechanical, not doctrinal. I could not tell what V and the 'after-mode' were meant to show, because V did nothing in the morning. The rope-and-snake page notes its translation is a working one 'awaiting review', and I can't judge its accuracy myself.
- **s04:** Not preachy: the pot scene shows rather than tells, and the codex pages give Sanskrit, transliteration and a translation with a short plain gloss. The 'Other schools' page was a good surprise: it gives the Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita readings of 'tat tvam asi' and says plainly that the game takes Advaita's reading, which felt honest. Confusing bits: one translation is marked 'working translation ... awaiting review', and the pages cite 'RESEARCH.md', which a player cannot see; and that page mentions the snake on the rope before I met it. I cannot judge accuracy of the Sanskrit myself.
